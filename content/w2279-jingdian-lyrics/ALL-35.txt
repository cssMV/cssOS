## 1. shakespeare-bankside-en.txt
作品 8aee5eed-a7da-4341-a38e-b766bb512400   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Elizabethan consort music, lute and viol, madrigal counterpoint, slow ground bass, Renaissance chamber strings, candlelit and sombre, adagio]
[Vocal: countertenor lead over male consort chorus, English cathedral polyphony, close madrigal harmony, little vibrato]
[Language: English — Early Modern English register. One line of Old English incantation in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: Moonlight on the Bankside]
Moonlight is poured on the old playhouse floor,
the Thames goes black beneath the Bankside stair.
A thousand groundlings gone, and gone their roar —
only the wind rehearses in the air.
Speak, and the empty galleries will hear.

[Verse 2: A Voice in the Dark Corner]
In the dark corner someone hums a line
he never wrote down, never meant to keep.
Old April crowds the heart like watered wine;
the dream of spring lies shallow in his sleep.
We are such stuff as dreams are made on — sleep.

[Chorus 1: Not Marble]
Not marble, nor the gilded monuments —
ink is the harder stone, the longer year.
Each word you set is hung among the firmaments,
each line you leave is lit, and still burns here.
Shakespeare, your soul of verse is standing clear.

[Verse 3: Fortune's Wheel]
Fortune turns out her wheel and will not stay;
she lifts the crown and lets the crown go down.
Like as the waves make toward the pebbled bay,
so come our minutes, breaking on the town.
The tide takes playhouse, poet, prince and clown.

[Verse 4: The Fire of 1613]
A cannon fired in Henry caught the thatch;
the Globe went up in one bright hour of June.
They raised it twice. No fire could keep the latch
on what was spoken there by afternoon.
Timber may fall. The speaking does not swoon.

[Chorus 2: Not Marble]
Not marble, nor the gilded monuments —
ink is the harder stone, the longer year.
Each word you set is hung among the firmaments,
each line you leave is lit, and still burns here.
Shakespeare, your soul of verse is standing clear.

[Bridge: Erce, Mother of Earth — the oldest charm in our tongue]
[Old English incantation, Aecerbot field charm, 11th-century manuscript]
Erce, Erce, Erce, eorthan modor —
hal wes thu, folde, fira modor.
Older than sonnets, older than the stage,
this was the English that we whispered first,
and all your lines are grafted on that root.

[Chorus 3: Not Marble]
Not marble, nor the gilded monuments —
ink is the harder stone, the longer year.
Each word you set is hung among the firmaments,
each line you leave is lit, and still burns here.
Shakespeare, your soul of verse is standing clear.

[Chorus 4: Not of an Age]
Heminges and Condell gathered up the leaves,
and Jonson wrote: not of an age — for all time.
Two friends, one folio; the century believes
because two players would not let it climb
into the dark unprinted. Keep the rhyme.

[Outro: Holy Trinity, Stratford]
At the end of the night, one old lament again.
Curst be he that moves my bones, you said,
and no man has. The chancel holds the stone.
Sleep, Warwickshire. The words have gone from home
and walk in every language but your own.

## 2. shakespeare-stage-en.txt
作品 71652034-0072-424d-9d1c-475e0fdd2bad   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Elizabethan broadside ballad, viol consort and recorder, pastoral Renaissance strings, slow 6/8 pavane, moonlit and valedictory, largo]
[Vocal: solo tenor with a small mixed consort answering, English madrigal tuning, plain and unornamented]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English incantation in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: Under a Quiet Sky]
Under a quiet sky the stars come out
above the Avon and the sleeping mill.
I call, and softly, to a dream far out —
a boy who walked to London and stood still
at the one door that let a nobody in.

[Verse 2: Walking Alone by Moonlight]
Walking alone by moonlight down the lane,
a thousand thoughts go touching at the past:
the glover's bench, the schoolroom and the cane,
the names of men whose names I made to last.
I touch the shadow. Shadows do not hold.

[Chorus 1: All the World's a Stage]
All the world's a stage, and all the men
and women merely players — so you wrote.
One ancient air, ten thousand years again;
we mourn the glory as we mourn the smoke.
Exits and entrances. And then the dark.

[Verse 3: Seven Ages]
First the mewling infant, then the boy,
the lover sighing like a furnace, then
the soldier swearing at a borrowed joy,
the justice with his capon and his pen,
and last, sans teeth, sans eyes, sans everything.

[Verse 4: The Years Go Like a Shuttle]
The years go like a shuttle through the loom;
the river carries off what it is lent.
I look up at the whole lit span of room
and count the fires that will not be re-lit.
Golden lads and girls all must come to dust.

[Chorus 2: All the World's a Stage]
All the world's a stage, and all the men
and women merely players — so you wrote.
One ancient air, ten thousand years again;
we mourn the glory as we mourn the smoke.
Exits and entrances. And then the dark.

[Bridge: Hlude Waeran Hy — against a sudden stitch]
[Old English incantation, Wid Faerstice, Lacnunga manuscript, tenth century]
Hlude waeran hy, la, hlude,
tha hy ofer thone hlaew ridan.
Older than the stage and older than the rhyme,
this was the English that we whispered first
when something none could see took us in the side.

[Chorus 3: All the World's a Stage]
All the world's a stage, and all the men
and women merely players — so you wrote.
One ancient air, ten thousand years again;
we mourn the glory as we mourn the smoke.
Exits and entrances. And then the dark.

[Chorus 4: The Melody Like Wind]
A melody goes through me like a wind
across a field I will not cross again.
There is a road back home I cannot find;
the way is a country with no living men.
Our revels now are ended. Let them end.

[Outro: Calling Softly Still]
Under a quiet sky the stars come out.
I call, and softly, and the dream is wide —
the memory has no end, and no last word,
only the players coming on and off
and someone in the dark who knows the lines.

## 3. shakespeare-hamlet-en.txt
作品 472bc848-6852-432d-8f23-e59dd5eca42a   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Elizabethan tragic consort, low viols and a single sackbut, Dorian mode, bell tolling under the choruses, graveyard stillness breaking into storm, lento]
[Vocal: dark tenor lead with a low male chorus answering as the Ghost, spoken-close on the verses, no ornament]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English incantation in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: Hamnet, August 1596]
A boy of eleven buried in the nave,
and the father two days' ride away, on tour.
Judith lived. Her twin went in the grave.
No letter. Nothing written. Not a word
for four years, and then a prince with his name.

[Verse 2: The Ghost on the Battlement]
They say he played the Ghost himself, at night,
in the cold scene before the drums begin —
a father walking, armoured, out of sight,
who tells his son the murder and goes in.
Remember me. He wrote that for himself.

[Chorus 1: To Be, or Not to Be]
To be, or not to be — that is the question:
whether to bear the arrows of the time
or take arms up against a sea of troubles.
The undiscovered country has no rhyme.
Thus conscience makes us cowards. And we stay.

[Verse 3: Wittenberg]
He sent the prince to a university
he never saw, to read a book of doubt.
A student with a skull and a theory
and thirty lines to get his father out.
He reasons for four acts. Then it is spring.

[Verse 4: Alas, Poor Yorick]
A gravedigger throws up a jester's head
and the prince holds it and begins to speak
of lips he kissed. Where be your gibes now, said
the man who made a living making jokes
for people who were going in the ground.

[Chorus 2: To Be, or Not to Be]
To be, or not to be — that is the question:
whether to bear the arrows of the time
or take arms up against a sea of troubles.
The undiscovered country has no rhyme.
Thus conscience makes us cowards. And we stay.

[Bridge: Ic Me On Thisse Gyrde Beluce]
[Old English incantation, the Journey Charm, eleventh-century manuscript]
Ic me on thisse gyrde beluce
and on Godes helde bebeode.
I lock myself inside this rod, and give
myself into God's keeping — that was English
before the stage, and it was for the road.

[Chorus 3: To Be, or Not to Be]
To be, or not to be — that is the question:
whether to bear the arrows of the time
or take arms up against a sea of troubles.
The undiscovered country has no rhyme.
Thus conscience makes us cowards. And we stay.

[Chorus 4: The Rest Is Silence]
Eight bodies on the boards and nobody left
to tell it but a soldier and a friend.
Horatio, report me and my cause,
and let the rest of it be silence. End.
The drum goes out. They carry him like a soldier.

[Outro: Stratford, the Chancel]
John Shakespeare went under in sixteen-one;
the son was in the city, writing Hamlet.
A glover and a player and a ghost,
three men, one grief, one costume, and one armour.
Good night, sweet prince. The flights of angels now.

## 4. shakespeare-sonnets-en.txt
作品 ce4e4c0b-62a4-4657-be2c-7a41311081db   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Elizabethan lute song, solo lute and bass viol, John Dowland melancholy, intimate chamber air, one voice and one instrument, very slow]
[Vocal: solo countertenor, close-miked and unaccompanied in places, English air-song diction, almost no vibrato]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English incantation in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: Mr W.H.]
A quarto nobody asked him to print,
Thomas Thorpe, sixteen hundred and nine.
To the only begetter of these sonnets,
Mister W.H. — and not a further line.
Four hundred years of men guessing a name.

[Verse 2: One Hundred and Twenty-Six]
The first long hundred go to a young man:
be married, have a child, outlive the glass.
Then stop insisting, then begin to plan
how ink outlasts a body. Then, at last,
the tenderness that nobody explains.

[Chorus 1: Shall I Compare Thee]
Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate.
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
and summer's lease hath all too short a date —
so long as men can breathe, this gives thee life.

[Verse 3: My Mistress' Eyes]
And then a woman, and the tone goes hard:
my mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun,
her breath reeks, and her hair is black as wire,
and I love her, and I will say so. Done.
No goddess. Nobody had written that.

[Verse 4: Th'Expense of Spirit]
Th'expense of spirit in a waste of shame
is lust in action — and before, a liar,
and after, hated. He put his own name
through that one, and he knew. Past reason, dear,
hunted, and had, and hated. Then again.

[Chorus 2: Shall I Compare Thee]
Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate.
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
and summer's lease hath all too short a date —
so long as men can breathe, this gives thee life.

[Bridge: Sitte Ge, Sigewif]
[Old English incantation, the Charm for a Swarm of Bees, tenth century]
Sitte ge, sigewif, sigath to eorthan,
naefre ge wilde to wuda fleogan.
Settle, you victory-women, sink to earth,
never fly wild into the wood again.
A charm to keep the sweetness in the hive.

[Chorus 3: Shall I Compare Thee]
Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate.
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
and summer's lease hath all too short a date —
so long as men can breathe, this gives thee life.

[Chorus 4: Not Mine Own Fears]
The years go past like water through a mill
and nothing of the flesh of it survives.
Not mine own fears, nor the prophetic soul
of the wide world dreaming on things to come —
can tell me how long any of this stays.

[Outro: In Black Ink]
One hundred fifty-four, and then no more.
He never spoke about them. Not a note,
no dedication of his own, no door
left open on the question. Only this:
in black ink still my love may still shine bright.

## 5. shakespeare-macbeth-en.txt
作品 363df566-62b5-4fdb-81db-85b7ee43e4cc   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Jacobean tragic orchestra, low strings and timpani with a shawm wailing over them, Aeolian mode, witch-chant rhythm in the choruses, dark and driving, allegro agitato]
[Vocal: baritone lead with three high female voices answering as the weird sisters, chanted and metallic, no vibrato]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English incantation in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: A Scottish King on the English Throne]
A king came down from Scotland with a book
he wrote himself on demons and their law,
and the Gunpowder men had just been took
and quartered at the Palace Yard. He saw
witches in everything. So we wrote witches.

[Verse 2: Upon the Heath]
Three women on a blasted heath with news
a soldier wanted badly, badly told:
all hail, that shalt be king. And then he chews
on it for twenty lines and he is sold.
They never lied to him. They only named it.

[Chorus 1: Tomorrow, and Tomorrow]
Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow,
creeps in this petty pace from day to day
to the last syllable of recorded time;
and all our yesterdays have lit the way
to dusty death. Out, out, brief candle. Out.

[Verse 3: Out, Damned Spot]
A woman walking in her sleep with light,
rubbing a hand that has been clean for weeks.
Who would have thought the old man had so much
blood in him. All the perfumes of Arabia
will not sweeten this little hand. Go, wash.

[Verse 4: Birnam Wood]
No man of woman born — and then a man
cut from his mother sideways, in the dark.
The wood will never move — and then it can,
because an army carries branches. Mark
how every promise kept him and still killed him.

[Chorus 2: Tomorrow, and Tomorrow]
Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow,
creeps in this petty pace from day to day
to the last syllable of recorded time;
and all our yesterdays have lit the way
to dusty death. Out, out, brief candle. Out.

[Bridge: Her Com Ingangan]
[Old English incantation, Wid Dweorh, against a fever-spirit, tenth century]
Her com ingangan in spidenwiht,
haefde him his haman on handa.
Here came a creeping spider-thing inside,
carrying his harness in his hand.
That was the English nightmare. We only staged it.

[Chorus 3: Tomorrow, and Tomorrow]
Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow,
creeps in this petty pace from day to day
to the last syllable of recorded time;
and all our yesterdays have lit the way
to dusty death. Out, out, brief candle. Out.

[Chorus 4: The Porter at the Gate]
And in the middle of it, someone knocks,
and a drunk porter comes to let them in
and makes three jokes about the gate of hell.
That is the whole machine: a laugh, a sin,
a door, a man with a lantern, and the murder.

[Outro: The Shortest of the Tragedies]
Two thousand lines, the shortest of the four,
and not one scene in it you can forget.
They say the players will not speak its name
inside a theatre. Call it the Scottish play.
The candle's out. Walk home. Say nothing more.

## 6. shakespeare-agincourt-en.txt
作品 4dc47b14-c6a0-41b2-b5a4-340b026a9776   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: English Renaissance martial, natural trumpets and side drums with viol consort, Mixolydian mode, processional building to a full brass chorale, allegro marziale]
[Vocal: heroic tenor lead with a male soldiers chorus answering in unison, declamatory, open-throat]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English battle-verse in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: A Muse of Fire]
O for a Muse of fire that would ascend
the brightest heaven of invention —
a kingdom for a stage, princes to act,
and monarchs to behold the swelling scene.
We have a wooden circle and some flags.

[Verse 2: This Wooden O]
Can this cockpit hold the fields of France?
Or may we cram within this wooden O
the very casques that did affright the air
at Agincourt? Then let us, and suppose
one man a thousand. Piece out our faults with thought.

[Chorus 1: We Few, We Happy Few]
We few, we happy few, we band of brothers;
for he today that sheds his blood with me
shall be my brother. Be he ne'er so vile,
this day shall gentle his condition. See —
the colours going up. And hold the line.

[Verse 3: Saint Crispin's Day]
The twenty-fifth of October, fourteen fifteen,
in the ploughed mud and rain at Maisoncelle:
six thousand English, archers in the rain,
and thirty thousand horse in a narrow field
that would not let a knight turn round and live.

[Verse 4: Falstaff Dies Offstage]
The fat man does not come. A woman says
the king has killed his heart, and that is all.
Three plays of laughter finished in a phrase
by a hostess in a doorway. Then the wall
of France. No comedy survives a crown.

[Chorus 2: We Few, We Happy Few]
We few, we happy few, we band of brothers;
for he today that sheds his blood with me
shall be my brother. Be he ne'er so vile,
this day shall gentle his condition. See —
the colours going up. And hold the line.

[Bridge: Her Aethelstan Cyning]
[Oldest English battle-verse, The Battle of Brunanburh, Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, 937]
Her Aethelstan cyning, eorla dryhten,
geslogon aet saecce sweorda ecgum.
Here Athelstan the king, lord of earls,
struck in the battle with the edges of swords.
We were singing war in English long before.

[Chorus 3: We Few, We Happy Few]
We few, we happy few, we band of brothers;
for he today that sheds his blood with me
shall be my brother. Be he ne'er so vile,
this day shall gentle his condition. See —
the colours going up. And hold the line.

[Chorus 4: The King Walks the Camp]
At night he borrows a cloak and walks the lines
and argues with his own men about the cause,
and loses the argument, and says nothing,
and goes aside and asks what a king is.
Upon the king. Let us our lives lay on.

[Outro: The Chorus Bows]
Thus far with rough and all-unable pen
our bending author hath pursued the story.
Small time, but in that small most greatly lived
this star of England. Now the glory goes
and France is lost again. Admit the Chorus out.

## 7. shakespeare-prospero-en.txt
作品 05a2455e-b075-4a56-a4e6-74031b0f8510   语言 en
==============================================================================

[Style: Late Jacobean masque, consort of recorders and a chamber organ with a solo lute, Blackfriars indoor acoustics, serene and dissolving, adagio, ending unaccompanied]
[Vocal: aging baritone lead, spoken-close and unhurried, a thin high choir fading behind him, no ornament]
[Language: English — Early Modern register. One line of Old English banishing charm in the Bridge.]

[Verse 1: Blackfriars, 1611]
A roofed hall now, and candles, and a price
that keeps the groundlings out and brings the court.
Music between the acts. A quieter voice
will carry here. The writing can be short
and strange, and nobody will call for blood.

[Verse 2: The Island]
An old man on an island with a book,
a daughter who has never seen a man,
a spirit he has promised twice to free
and keeps for one more errand, one more plan.
Twelve years of exile managed like a stage.

[Chorus 1: Now My Charms Are All O'erthrown]
Now my charms are all o'erthrown,
and what strength I have's mine own,
which is most faint. Release me from my bands
with the help of your good hands.
Let your indulgence set me free.

[Verse 3: Break My Staff]
I'll break my staff, bury it fathoms deep,
and deeper than did ever plummet sound
I'll drown my book. The thing that let me keep
the weather and the dead men off the ground —
I put it in the sea. On purpose. Done.

[Verse 4: Two Noble Kinsmen]
And then a younger man came in to write
the last two with me: Fletcher, and his hand
is plain in every other scene. All right.
I took the credit and I took the stand
of a man who has somewhere else to be.

[Chorus 2: Now My Charms Are All O'erthrown]
Now my charms are all o'erthrown,
and what strength I have's mine own,
which is most faint. Release me from my bands
with the help of your good hands.
Let your indulgence set me free.

[Bridge: Wenne, Wenne, Wenchichenne]
[Old English banishing charm, Wid Wennum, against a growth, eleventh century]
Wenne, wenne, wenchichenne,
her ne scealt thu timbrien, ne nenne tun habban.
Wen, wen, little wen, you shall not build
here, nor have any homestead here at all.
The oldest English word for: go. Be gone.

[Chorus 3: Now My Charms Are All O'erthrown]
Now my charms are all o'erthrown,
and what strength I have's mine own,
which is most faint. Release me from my bands
with the help of your good hands.
Let your indulgence set me free.
